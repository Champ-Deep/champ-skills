# Audience Buckets: Reading the Case Study by Buyer Type

The same set of facts can win or lose depending on who reads them. A CFO and a CMO reading
the same case study are asking entirely different questions. If you do not pick a primary
audience and write for them, you end up writing for no one.

This file maps the primary buyer buckets for B2B services (data, outreach, marketing, events)
to what they scan for, what makes them share it, and how to structure the case study for impact.

---

## How to Use This File

After completing verification, ask the user: "Who is the primary reader of this case study?
The person most likely to share it internally or use it to make a purchase decision?"

Then come back here, find the matching bucket, and apply the lens to the narrative.

You can write for a primary audience without alienating others. The technique is to lead with
the primary audience's concern, then address secondarily. The headline and stat callouts do
the heavy lifting — make those speak to the primary reader.

---

## Bucket 1: The CMO / VP Marketing

**Primary concern:** ROI on marketing spend, pipeline contribution, and being seen as
driving revenue-aligned programs — not just generating activity.

**What they scan for first:** A headline metric that proves marketing spend generated
business outcomes. Response rate, pipeline generated, or qualified meetings booked.

**What makes them share it internally:** "This proves we can justify the budget." Anything
that quantifies marketing's contribution to revenue in language the CEO or CFO will accept.

**Vocabulary that lands:** Pipeline, conversion, ICP match, qualified leads, revenue attribution,
campaign performance, GTM efficiency.

**Structure emphasis for this buyer:**
- Open with the ROI or pipeline metric
- Spend more time on the "before" state — what wasn't working and why
- Include the CMO/VP at the named client in the narrative or quote (peer credibility)
- Close with a forward-looking statement about what they built together

**Tone:** Peer-to-peer, confident, numbers-forward. They are sophisticated buyers who see
through puffery. Be direct about what drove the outcome.

---

## Bucket 2: The CEO / Founder

**Primary concern:** Growth rate, competitive positioning, and efficient use of resources.
They are buying conviction, not product specs. They want to know if this worked for a company
like theirs.

**What they scan for first:** The company profile (size, industry, stage) and the headline
outcome. "This company is like mine, and they got this result."

**What makes them share it:** "This is exactly what we've been talking about doing." CEOs
share things that validate a direction they are already leaning toward.

**Vocabulary that lands:** Growth, scale, speed, efficiency, competitive advantage, market
penetration, high-value prospects.

**Structure emphasis for this buyer:**
- Open with a brief company profile that mirrors their world
- Lead with the biggest, most impressive metric
- Keep the "how we did it" section short — they trust you figured it out
- Emphasize what happened after: expansion, momentum, follow-on business
- A direct CEO-to-CEO quote from the client is the single most powerful element here

**Tone:** Strategic, outcome-first, no jargon. Founders skim; make every sentence earn its place.

---

## Bucket 3: The CIO / Head of Technology

**Primary concern:** Data quality, system compatibility, reliability, and risk mitigation.
They approve purchases that touch data infrastructure. They are skeptical of claims and
detail-oriented.

**What they scan for first:** How the data was sourced, validated, and delivered. What
was the quality assurance process? Were there issues, and how were they handled?

**What makes them share it:** "This vendor actually knows what they are talking about."
Technical credibility signals: specific data specs, validation methodology, delivery process.

**Vocabulary that lands:** Data integrity, accuracy rate, verification methodology, refresh
cadence, compliance, data schema, CXO segment coverage, delivery SLA.

**Structure emphasis for this buyer:**
- Open with the challenge in technical terms (bad data, low match rates, compliance risk)
- Give the most detail on the solution phase — more than for any other bucket
- Include a specific data quality proof point (sample approval, accuracy rate, match rate)
- Close with reliability/consistency signals: "second engagement," "expanded scope," "renewed"

**Tone:** Precise, methodical, no superlatives. Claims should be traceable. Avoid marketing
speak entirely — it erodes credibility with this reader.

---

## Bucket 4: The Sales Leader / VP Sales / BDR Manager

**Primary concern:** Pipeline quantity and quality. How many qualified conversations did
this generate, and how fast? They care about velocity above all else.

**What they scan for first:** Response rate, meeting rate, qualified lead count, and speed
to result. If you can show "X meetings booked in Y weeks," you have them.

**What makes them share it:** "This is ammunition I can use in a budget conversation with
my VP." Anything that translates directly into meetings, pipeline, or quota attainment.

**Vocabulary that lands:** Qualified leads, meeting booked, pipeline generated, response
rate, outreach velocity, conversion, prospecting, ICP fit, warm handoffs.

**Structure emphasis for this buyer:**
- Lead with the pipeline/activity metric in the headline
- Emphasize speed — how fast did results appear?
- Show the process briefly: what list, what message, what result
- Include any sales cycle acceleration or deal size correlation if available

**Tone:** Direct, numbers-heavy, no fluff. This reader does not have time. Get to the metric
in the first three sentences.

---

## Bucket 5: The Procurement / Finance Approver

**Primary concern:** Value for money, risk mitigation, and vendor credibility. They are
rarely the champion — they are the gatekeeper who can kill a deal if they are not addressed.

**What they scan for first:** Cost-outcome relationship, vendor track record, and whether
the claims are substantiated. They are looking for red flags, not green flags.

**What makes them approve:** "This vendor has done this before for credible companies, the
claims are documented, and the outcome is defensible." They share case studies with colleagues
only when they are comfortable being associated with the recommendation.

**Vocabulary that lands:** ROI, cost per lead, risk management, documented results, verified
data, established process, client retention.

**Structure emphasis for this buyer:**
- Keep it short. One page. They skim.
- Lead with a named, credible client if possible — brand recognition reduces their risk perception
- Include the metric AND the source ("per campaign tracking," "per Sean's delivery records")
- Close with any evidence of ongoing/repeated engagement (shows the relationship holds)

**Tone:** Conservative, factual, credibility-first. Do not oversell. Understated claims that
are provable beat impressive claims that raise doubt.

---

## Bucket 6: The Event / Community Organizer

**Primary concern:** Turnout, audience quality, and partner credibility. This buyer appears
primarily in event-adjacent case studies (data for event outreach, venue support, event promotion).

**What they scan for first:** Attendance outcome or outreach reach. How many people showed up,
or how many were targeted? Was the audience the right profile?

**What makes them share it:** "This is exactly the vendor conversation I need to have before
my next event."

**Vocabulary that lands:** Registered attendees, RSVP rate, CXO attendance, curated audience,
outreach precision, event data, contact coverage.

**Structure emphasis:**
- Lead with the event outcome (X executives registered, Y% attendance rate)
- Show the outreach pipeline: contacts targeted > outreach sent > responses > registrations
- Include venue or event quality signals if relevant (premium venue = premium positioning)

**Tone:** Enthusiastic but precise. Event buyers are often experiential thinkers — help them
visualize the room.

---

## Multi-Audience Case Studies

Sometimes a case study will circulate across several of these buyers. When that is the case:

1. Choose a primary audience for the headline and stat callouts.
2. Write the narrative body to serve the primary audience.
3. In the "Solution" section, include one sentence that speaks to the secondary buyer's concern.
4. In the full PDF, the "Impact" section can include a subsection that quantifies both the
   marketing outcome and the operational/efficiency angle simultaneously.

Do not try to write for everyone at once. Pick one primary lens and hold it.
