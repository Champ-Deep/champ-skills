# The IMPACT Narrative Framework

Most B2B case studies read like product documentation. They describe what was sold, list some
numbers, and end with a quote. The prospect skims it, learns nothing about themselves, and closes
the tab.

The IMPACT framework is built around a different principle: **the case study is not about your
product. It is about the client's transformation.** Your product is the vehicle. Their outcome is
the destination. Write toward the destination.

Each section answers a question the prospect is silently asking while reading. If you answer those
questions well, you do not need to sell. The story sells for you.

---

## The Six Sections

### I — Identify the Friction

**The silent question:** "Was this client's situation anything like mine?"

This is the "before" world. Paint it specifically and honestly. Do not sanitize it — the more
real the friction feels, the more powerfully the resolution will land. The prospect needs to
recognise themselves in this section.

**What to include:**
- What was the client trying to do that wasn't working?
- What was the cost of that friction — time, missed revenue, competitive disadvantage?
- What had they already tried (if anything)?
- How long had they been living with this problem?

**Tone:** Empathetic. You are not judging the client's previous situation. You are acknowledging
that the problem was real and the stakes were real.

**Common mistake:** Opening with company background. Nobody cares that the client "is a leading
provider of [X]." Open with the problem.

**Example opening:**
> Before engaging with SPAN, the team at [Company] was running outreach to a list they had built
> manually over two years. Open rates had dropped to below 4%. Their SDR was spending three hours
> a day on research just to personalise twenty emails. Pipeline was drying up.

---

### M — Moment of Decision

**The silent question:** "Why did they choose you specifically?"

This is often the shortest section, but do not skip it. It answers the implicit objection: "Why
not a competitor? Why not do it in-house?" The decision story validates the choice and subtly
positions you against alternatives.

**What to include:**
- What triggered the decision to act now?
- What were they looking for in a solution?
- Why did they choose you (or what was the tipping point)?
- Was there a specific proof point, referral, or conversation that convinced them?

**Tone:** Matter-of-fact, not boastful. Let the logic of their decision speak for itself.

**Example:**
> After reviewing three data providers, [Company] chose SPAN primarily because of the depth of
> title-level targeting available for their target vertical — mid-market manufacturing companies
> with 50–500 employees. What sealed it was the ability to pull a test sample before committing.

---

### P — Proof in Motion

**The silent question:** "What exactly did you do? How does it actually work?"

This is the execution section. Be specific. Do not say "we provided a customised data solution."
Say what kind of data, how many records, targeting parameters, how the campaign ran, what channels
were used, what cadence was applied. Specificity is credibility.

**What to include:**
- The exact product or service delivered (list size, targeting spec, campaign setup)
- The actions taken in sequence (what happened first, then next)
- Any customisation or unique approach specific to this client
- What the client's team did with what you provided (if relevant)

**Tone:** Practical and confident. This is your methodology on display. It should feel replicable.

**Example:**
> SPAN delivered a contact list of 3,200 verified decision-maker contacts matching [Company]'s
> ICP: VP-level and above at manufacturing companies in the US Midwest, 50–500 employees,
> using an ERP or WMS system. The list was matched against 6-month intent signals for "supply
> chain software" and "inventory management."
>
> [Company]'s SDR team ran a 5-touch email sequence over 4 weeks, with personalisation fields
> pre-populated using the enriched data. SPAN provided two subject line variants for A/B testing.

---

### A — Actual Outcomes

**The silent question:** "Did it actually work? Show me the numbers."

This is the emotional climax of the case study. Everything built to this. Lead with the strongest
number first. Then contextualise it. Then add secondary metrics if available.

**What to include:**
- Primary metric (the most impressive, most specific number)
- Context (industry benchmark, previous baseline, or time period)
- Secondary metrics (supporting evidence)
- Any qualitative outcome (the team reaction, the VP's response, the deal that was won)

**Tone:** Direct. Let the numbers carry the weight. Resist the urge to editorialize ("incredible
results!") — the number speaks louder than your adjective.

**Formatting tip:** In both the on-page and PDF versions, this section benefits from visual
callout boxes. E.g.: "62 qualified leads in 8 weeks" in large type, followed by context.

**Example:**
> In 8 weeks from list delivery, [Company] generated 62 qualified leads — a 4.2x increase over
> their previous 8-week baseline of 15 leads. Response rate on the email campaign hit 11.3%,
> compared to a 2.8% industry average for cold outreach in the manufacturing vertical.
>
> Three of those leads converted to closed deals within the same quarter, representing $218,000
> in new ARR.

---

### C — Cascade Effect

**The silent question:** "What did the results actually enable? What happens next for them?"

Most case studies stop at the metric. The cascade is what makes a metric meaningful. A "62 leads"
metric becomes transformative when you understand that it allowed the company to hire two more
SDRs, or hit their Series A milestone, or expand into a new vertical. The cascade connects the
data point to real business trajectory.

**What to include:**
- What did the results enable the client to do?
- How has their engagement with you changed (renewals, expanded spend, new products)?
- What does their future look like because of this outcome?

**Tone:** Forward-looking, positive, human. This is where you show that you are not just a
data vendor — you are a business catalyst.

**Example:**
> Off the back of that pipeline, [Company] expanded their outreach budget for Q3, purchasing a
> second dataset targeting West Coast accounts in the same vertical. The SDR team now runs
> continuous outreach rather than batch-and-blast campaigns. Their cost per qualified lead dropped
> from $340 to $91.

---

### T — Testimony

**The silent question:** "Is this real? Would the client actually say this worked?"

The quote is the trust transfer. The prospect does not fully trust you (yet). But they extend
a degree of trust to someone who has already been in their position. A good quote does not just
say "they were great to work with." It says something specific about what changed.

**What to include:**
- A direct quote from the client (name, title, company if named)
- Ideally: the quote references the transformation, not just the service
- If no formal quote is available: a paraphrased summary marked as such, pending confirmation

**What a weak quote looks like:**
> "SPAN was great to work with and delivered quality data."

**What a strong quote looks like:**
> "Before SPAN, our outreach felt like shouting into a void. With the right contacts and the intent
> data layered in, our SDR actually started enjoying his day. We went from dreading the pipeline
> review to looking forward to it."
> — [Name, Title, Company]

If you do not have a quote yet, flag this clearly in the output and include a placeholder. Then
instruct the user to go back to CS for a testimonial before publishing.

## Narrative Principles Summary

| Principle | Application |
|-----------|-------------|
| Lead with their world, not your product | First paragraph is always about the client's situation |
| Specificity is credibility | Never write "improved significantly" when you can write "14% response rate" |
| Numbers are the climax, not the opener | Build toward the data through story, then land it hard |
| The "before" must be visceral | If the reader doesn't feel the friction, the resolution won't land |
| The cascade makes metrics meaningful | Connect the number to what it actually enabled |
| The client's voice > your voice | One genuine client quote outweighs two paragraphs of your copy |
| Anonymous works, named is better | Push for named. Offer to review the draft with the client first. |

## Word Count Guidance

| Section | On-Page Version | Full PDF Version |
|---------|-----------------|------------------|
| I — Identify the Friction | 40-60 words | 80-120 words |
| M — Moment of Decision | 20-30 words | 40-60 words |
| P — Proof in Motion | 50-80 words | 100-150 words |
| A — Actual Outcomes | 30-50 words + 3-4 stat callouts | 80-120 words + stat callouts |
| C — Cascade Effect | Optional (30 words if included) | 60-100 words |
| T — Testimony | 1-2 sentences | Full quote with attribution |
| **Total** | **~300-400 words + stats** | **~600-800 words + stats** |
